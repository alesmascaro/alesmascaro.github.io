2  Related work

2.1 From IDA to effect inference under graph uncertainty

Pearl’s graphical framework connects conditional independence, structural equations and interventions (Pearl 1988, 2009). With observational data and the usual causal sufficiency and faithfulness assumptions, a discovery procedure can identify a Markov equivalence class rather than a unique causal DAG. The IDA algorithm of Maathuis, Kalisch and Bühlmann addresses the resulting effect ambiguity: it combines an estimated equivalence class with local parent-set possibilities to estimate a collection of possible intervention effects in linear systems (Maathuis et al. 2009). These possibilities are not posterior probabilities, and repeated estimates need not represent distinct causal functionals.

Several branches develop this starting point. Joint-IDA extends effect estimation to simultaneous interventions, where intervening on one treatment changes paths from another (Nandy et al. 2017). Ancestral-graph methods extend possible-effect reasoning to settings that allow latent variables, under different graphical assumptions (Malinsky and Spirtes 2016). Neither extension can be obtained by applying a single-treatment, causally sufficient formula unchanged.

The adjustment branch asks when an observational regression identifies a causal effect across compatible graphs. Perković, Textor, Kalisch and Maathuis give a complete graphical characterization of adjustment sets for DAGs and several partially directed or ancestral graph classes (Perković et al. 2018). Incorporating background knowledge leads to maximally oriented partially directed acyclic graphs (MPDAGs), for which Perković develops a broader identification criterion (Perković 2020). Identification by the g-formula can be possible even when adjustment alone is insufficient. Guo and Perković further partition a graph class by distinct identified effect functionals, reducing duplication in enumeration (Guo and Perković 2021).

A second branch concerns efficiency after identification. Henckel, Perković and Maathuis establish graphical variance comparisons and the O-set’s asymptotic optimality among valid least-squares adjustment estimators in causal linear models (Henckel et al. 2022). Witte, Henckel, Maathuis and Didelez interpret this set through forbidden projection and use it for efficient estimation of possible effects (Witte et al. 2020). These findings motivate the outcome-parent set in the I-MAP construction. Their optimality is within an adjustment-estimator class: Guo and Perković show that recursive least squares can exploit additional linear-model restrictions and is efficient among regular estimators based on the sample covariance (Guo and Perković 2022). Guo, Perković and Rotnitzky study elimination of variables while retaining identification and the semiparametric efficiency bound for an interventional mean (Guo et al. 2023).

Further developments address conditional effects and the causal consequences of discovery errors. LaPlante and Perković characterize conditional adjustment in MPDAGs and PAGs when conditioning variables are unaffected by treatment (LaPlante and Perković 2024). Their preprint on conditional identification goes beyond adjustment to a complete identification algorithm in MPDAGs (LaPlante and Perković 2025). A separate preprint by LaPlante, Triantafillou and Perković provides data-driven routes to adjustment for multiple treatments under restrictions on the adjustment variables (LaPlante et al. 2025). These results concern identification criteria, not automatic correction for all uncertainty introduced by estimating a graph from finite data.

Henckel, Würtzen and Weichwald propose adjustment identification distances that assess learned structures through the causal formulas they imply (Henckel et al. 2024). Wienöbst, Henckel and Weichwald develop discrete score-based search with iterative Cholesky updates (Wienöbst et al. 2026). Freni, Henckel and Weichwald’s verification preprint asks whether a given observational formula identifies an intervention distribution, a question distinct from finding some identifying formula (Freni et al. 2026). Together, these strands connect identification, efficient estimation and computational discovery. They do not supply a common prior on an effect across unknown DAGs, which is the inferential role considered here.

2.2 Bayesian structure learning and causal effect estimation

Bayesian methods give weights to graph and parameter uncertainty rather than only enumerating possible effects. Castelletti and Consonni develop Gaussian causal effect inference with unknown structure (Castelletti and Consonni 2021). Castelletti and Mascaro consider joint interventions among network-dependent variables (Castelletti and Mascaro 2021). Pensar and colleagues provide another Bayesian route to observational effect inference (Pensar et al. 2020), and Castelletti, Consonni and Della Vedova develop a categorical-model workflow (Castelletti et al. 2024).

For Gaussian models, DAG-Wishart priors provide conjugate integration and parameter sampling (Ben-David et al. 2015). Peluso and Consonni construct compatible priors for comparing DAG models (Peluso and Consonni 2020), building on the parameter-prior framework of Geiger and Heckerman (Geiger and Heckerman 2002). The resulting evidence and averaging calculations are explained in Chapter 4. Their practicality is a useful benchmark for an effect-centered alternative.

Non-local priors make prior density vanish at a null parameter value and can alter Bayes-factor learning rates under stated testing assumptions (Johnson and Rossell 2010). This motivates separating structural null effects from active effects and controlling the latter near zero. The exact reweighting identity in Section 6.6 is available for a normalized effect tilt; claims about graph-selection consistency or effect-estimation performance require additional analysis.

2.3 Frugal parameterization

Evans and Didelez organize a joint model around a causal distribution, together with a distribution for the remaining variables and a conditional association component (Evans and Didelez 2024). Under their assumptions, this yields a smooth, regular and non-redundant parameterization. Appropriate association measures, such as odds ratios or copulas, can supply variation independence. Their framework supports simulation and likelihood-based inference, including Bayesian inference, and extends beyond Gaussian mean effects to marginal causal distributions and longitudinal models.

Three properties need to be distinguished. Non-redundancy means that distinct admissible parameter values do not encode the same distribution. Variation independence means that parameter blocks can vary over a Cartesian product of their separate ranges. Saturation means that a model contains every distribution in the specified observational family. A parameterization can be non-redundant without being saturated or variation independent. In the paper’s Gaussian example, a regression coefficient for the association component entails a variance inequality; using an appropriate correlation removes that dependence in the saturated model (Evans and Didelez 2024, Example 1.2).

2.3.1 A sparse chain: constraints without redundancy

Analytical example. Consider the centered Gaussian chain

\[ X_1=\epsilon_1,\qquad X_2=bX_1+\epsilon_2,\qquad X_3=cX_2+\epsilon_3,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_j)=v_j>0, \]

with mutually independent errors. The target is the effect of 1 on 3, \(\tau_{13}=bc\). Write \(s=c^2v_2+v_3\). Since treatment 1 has no parents,

\[ X_3\mid\operatorname{do}(X_1=x)\sim N(\tau_{13}x,s) \]

also equals its observational conditional distribution given \(X_1=x\).

A Gaussian marginal-and-association representation of all three variables is

\[ X_1\sim N(0,v_1),\qquad X_2\mid X_1\sim N(bX_1,v_2), \]

\[ X_3\mid X_1,X_2\sim N\{\tau X_1+\alpha(X_2-bX_1),\ s-\alpha^2v_2\}. \]

Marginalizing over \(X_2\mid X_1\) gives \(X_3\mid X_1\sim N(\tau X_1,s)\). Thus \((v_1,b,v_2)\) describes \(p(x_1,x_2)\), \((\tau,s)\) describes the marginal effect model, and \(\alpha\) describes residual association. Here \(X_2\) is a mediator, not a pretreatment covariate: the integration weight is \(p(x_2\mid x_1)\), rather than a baseline marginal \(p(x_2)\). This is a marginal-and-association special case of the frugal construction; the usual pretreatment adjustment recipe cannot be used unchanged.

On the saturated Gaussian family, the domain is \(v_1,v_2,s>0\) and \(s>\alpha^2v_2\), with \(b,\tau\in\mathbb R\). Expanding the conditional mean shows that the direct coefficient of \(X_1\) in the outcome equation is \(\tau-\alpha b\). The chain’s missing edge \(1\to3\) therefore imposes exactly

\[ \tau=\alpha b. \]

On this constrained domain, the original coefficients and variances are recovered uniquely:

\[ c=\alpha,\qquad v_3=s-\alpha^2v_2. \]

The chain has five free parameters, and the six ambient coordinates satisfy one independent equation. There is no duplication of distributions. Ignoring the equation would fit the saturated model instead of the chain.

To separate this constraint from the variance inequality, use

\[ \rho=\alpha\sqrt{v_2/s},\qquad -1<\rho<1. \]

This is the correlation of \(X_2-bX_1\) with \(X_3-\tau X_1\). In the ambient model, \((v_1,b,v_2)\), \((\tau,s)\) and \(\rho\) have a Cartesian-product domain. On the chain, however,

\[ \tau=b\rho\sqrt{s/v_2},\qquad c=\rho\sqrt{s/v_2},\qquad v_3=s(1-\rho^2). \]

The restricted parameterization remains injective, but the effect cannot vary independently of these particular nuisance coordinates. If \(b=0\), the effect is necessarily zero. Where \(b\ne0\), \((\tau,b,v_1,v_2,v_3)\) gives an alternative chart, with \(c=\tau/b\) and absolute inverse Jacobian \(1/|b|\). That chart excludes valid points with \(b=0\); at \(b=c=0\), the differential of \(bc\) vanishes, so \(\tau\) cannot be a coordinate in a smooth local chart of the full five-dimensional model.

For the distinct target effect of 2 on 3, node 1 is a pretreatment variable. Applying the elementary frugal recipe with \(Z=X_1\), treatment \(X_2\) and outcome \(X_3\) sets the residual \(Z\)-outcome association to zero and retains the five free parameters. This gives another sparse, non-redundant example.

Consequently, a complete DAG is not necessary for non-redundancy. Missing edges restrict the admissible frugal coordinates, and may destroy their blockwise variation independence. The practical comparison with I-MAP coordinates concerns how these restrictions are constructed and handled across a graph search, rather than an inherent redundancy of frugal parameterization.

### A sparse chain: constraints without redundancy {#sec-frugal-chain}

**Analytical example.** Consider the centered Gaussian chain

$$
X_1=\epsilon_1,\qquad X_2=bX_1+\epsilon_2,\qquad
X_3=cX_2+\epsilon_3,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_j)=v_j>0,
$$

with mutually independent errors. The target is the effect of 1 on 3,
$\tau_{13}=bc$. Write $s=c^2v_2+v_3$. Since treatment 1 has no parents,

$$
X_3\mid\operatorname{do}(X_1=x)\sim N(\tau_{13}x,s)
$$

also equals its observational conditional distribution given $X_1=x$.

A Gaussian marginal-and-association representation of all three variables is

$$
X_1\sim N(0,v_1),\qquad X_2\mid X_1\sim N(bX_1,v_2),
$$

$$
X_3\mid X_1,X_2\sim
N\{\tau X_1+\alpha(X_2-bX_1),\ s-\alpha^2v_2\}.
$$

Marginalizing over $X_2\mid X_1$ gives $X_3\mid X_1\sim N(\tau X_1,s)$.
Thus $(v_1,b,v_2)$ describes $p(x_1,x_2)$, $(\tau,s)$ describes the marginal effect model, and $\alpha$ describes residual association. Here $X_2$ is a **mediator**, not a pretreatment covariate: the integration weight is $p(x_2\mid x_1)$, rather than a baseline marginal $p(x_2)$. This is a marginal-and-association special case of the frugal construction; the usual pretreatment adjustment recipe cannot be used unchanged.

On the saturated Gaussian family, the domain is $v_1,v_2,s>0$ and $s>\alpha^2v_2$, with $b,\tau\in\mathbb R$. Expanding the conditional mean shows that the direct coefficient of $X_1$ in the outcome equation is $\tau-\alpha b$. The chain's missing edge $1\to3$ therefore imposes exactly

$$
\tau=\alpha b.
$$

On this constrained domain, the original coefficients and variances are recovered uniquely:

$$
c=\alpha,\qquad v_3=s-\alpha^2v_2.
$$

The chain has five free parameters, and the six ambient coordinates satisfy one independent equation. There is no duplication of distributions. Ignoring the equation would fit the saturated model instead of the chain.

To separate this constraint from the variance inequality, use

$$
\rho=\alpha\sqrt{v_2/s},\qquad -1<\rho<1.
$$

This is the correlation of $X_2-bX_1$ with $X_3-\tau X_1$. In the ambient model, $(v_1,b,v_2)$, $(\tau,s)$ and $\rho$ have a Cartesian-product domain. On the chain, however,

$$
\tau=b\rho\sqrt{s/v_2},\qquad
c=\rho\sqrt{s/v_2},\qquad v_3=s(1-\rho^2).
$$

The restricted parameterization remains injective, but the effect cannot vary independently of these particular nuisance coordinates. If $b=0$, the effect is necessarily zero. Where $b\ne0$, $(\tau,b,v_1,v_2,v_3)$ gives an alternative chart, with $c=\tau/b$ and absolute inverse Jacobian $1/|b|$. That chart excludes valid points with $b=0$; at $b=c=0$, the differential of $bc$ vanishes, so $\tau$ cannot be a coordinate in a smooth local chart of the full five-dimensional model.

For the distinct target effect of 2 on 3, node 1 is a pretreatment variable. Applying the elementary frugal recipe with $Z=X_1$, treatment $X_2$ and outcome $X_3$ sets the residual $Z$-outcome association to zero and retains the five free parameters. This gives another sparse, non-redundant example.

Consequently, a complete DAG is **not necessary** for non-redundancy. Missing edges restrict the admissible frugal coordinates, and may destroy their blockwise variation independence.
